A Personal Note

Why I Picked Up the Pencil

My name is Omer. I was born in Mardin, a city where every stone has been prayed over by someone's faith and marched over by someone's army. All my life I have carried two instincts that refuse to leave each other alone: faith, which tells me the world means something, and logic, which demands to see the working. I never wanted to choose between them. I wanted to bind them.

And I carry a second habit, older than the first: the rescue everyone mentality. When I find a door, I do not want to walk through it alone. I want everyone I love through it before me. So whatever I find has to be checked, hard, because a rescue rope nobody has tested is not a rescue rope.

So I picked up the simplest tool a human being owns, a pencil, and I divided one span of years by twelve. This page is what fell out. I kept every number, kept every failure, and marked every trick my own mind played on me along the way.

What follows is a clock. I will open the case first, then take the movement apart piece by piece, from the gears down to the case back, showing at every part the clock in the sky it copies. Then I let the whole machine run, and walk you through every place its hands have landed. I explain this clock because my heritage requires me to explain it, and requires it of me now. Whose heritage, and why it commands this, is told at the very end, where a watchmaker signs his work. Nothing here needs mathematics. It only needs looking honestly at what is left over.

The Coincidence

The Core of the Miracle

Before the sky, before the watch, you should see the coincidence this whole book stands on. My pencil produced two step numbers, 239 and 258. I chose neither. Both turned out to be somebody.

239A prime, unbreakable. One tooth short of 240, the most divisible number of its size. And the number inside the formula that first computed one hundred digits of pi. The circle's own prime, reached by subtracting one year from another.
2582 × 3 × 43. Lay twelve of them end to end and my whole road fits exactly: 3096 = 12 × 258. The zodiac's clock closes without remainder.
3097One more year, and every atom changes: 3097 = 19 × 163. And 19 is not mine. It is the moon's: the treaty number after which sun and moon agree. 163 is the largest Heegner number, the deepest of the nine.

The pencil's numbers mesh with the moon's number. I put neither of them there. This is the core of the miracle, and every page that follows is me taking it apart to check that it is real.

The Watch

Inside the Case

A watch is two wheels and a promise. Open the back of mine: the balance swings, that is the oscillating circle itself; the brass gear ticks every 239 years and the green gear every 258; the hand runs backwards one position per brass tick; and the window reads the year. Click the crown to wind it.

The Train I

Two Gears

In the watch: the mesh. 239 against 258, no shared atom, marks realigning once in 61,662 teeth: the year 40,984, where the Machine ahead will carry you.

When two gears share an atom, they realign quickly. When they share nothing, they cannot meet again until every tooth has touched every tooth. 239 and 258 share nothing.

They pass one year apart, then the odometer runs: first true meeting at 40,984.
The Train II

The King Next Door

In the watch: the brass gear. 239 teeth, one short of the king 240. That missing tooth is the entire drift.

240 divides evenly more ways than any number near it. 239 lives one door down, so it is one-short on every one of the king's clocks at once. Touch the bars.

The race: hops of 240 come home every time. My 239 falls one year behind per hop: four hops, four years, four positions.
The Train III

Atoms

In the watch: the green gear. 258 splits into 2 x 3 x 43, so its motion can be geared down. The brass gear is an atom, and cannot.

Every number is built from unbreakable atoms called primes, in exactly one way, like a fingerprint. Whether a division works is decided entirely by which atoms are inside. Try any number.

One green hop split into its atoms, 2 x 3 x 43. The brass hop shakes and stays whole: 239 is an atom.
The Train IV

The Phase Engine

In the watch: the two gears. The sun hand and the moon hand are the same machine as my 239 and 258: two rates on one axle of time, and the phase is nothing but their angle. Every misfit pair of wheels is a moon factory.

The sun's wheel turns once in 365.2422 days, the moon's once in 29.5306. They do not divide: 12.37 moons per year. Every calendar humanity built is a treaty with this misfit. Watch the two hands run.

Left, the moon as the sky shows it. Right, the two hands that make it. The gold hand is the sun's year, the green hand is the moon's month, and the phase is nothing but the angle between them.

The Balance I

The Nineteen

In the watch: the balance. The Nineteen is the sky's oscillator: the swing after which sun and moon agree again, two hours short of perfect. That shortfall is why every clock ever built needs a regulator, and why every calendar needs a leap rule.

Run the two hands long enough and a whole number appears: 19 years is almost exactly 235 moons. The Metonic cycle, the reconciliation constant of sun and moon. Almost: each cycle slips by two hours, and my clock's ledger counted 163 of them.

Two rings filling together: the outer counts 19 years, the inner counts 235 moons. They close within two hours of each other.
The Balance II

The Sliver of Pi

In the watch: the balance. A sliver of angle is the balance's whole vocabulary. Four tilts, and the overshoot is arctan(1/239): pi hiding in the swing.

The balance speaks only in angles, and an angle is where my number was hiding all along. Here is the mathematical piece. Tilt a ray by the same small angle four times. You land a whisker past a perfect eighth of a turn, and the whisker is exactly one part in 239. Three centuries ago that sliver computed pi to one hundred digits by hand.

Four equal tilts overshoot the perfect eighth by one part in 239. My hop, hiding inside pi.
The Hand I

The Backward Clock

In the watch: the retrograde hand. This page is its instruction manual: plus 239, slip back one.

A step of 240 is exactly twenty laps of a 12 clock: you land where you started. A step of 239 is one hour short, so every step lands you one position behind. Step, slip back one.

The brass gear ticking: every tick lands one position back. 3, 2, 1, 0, 11.
The Hand II

The Shadow Engine

In the watch: the retrograde hand. The Saros is the hand that snaps back: the same sun, moon and node configuration returning to the same position on the dial, 6585.32 days per snap, the shadow arriving on schedule.

Eclipses keep their own books. The Saros: 6585.32 days, and the same shadow returns. My terminal eclipse's chain runs through Eddington's 1919. And its 19 year Metonic sibling crossed China in 2008, one day off the same calendar date.

The Saros 136 chain, hopping 18 years 11 days at a time, with the Metonic pair 2008 to 2027 drawn beneath it in green: nineteen years, one day.
The Axle

The Mirror Trick

In the watch: the axle. Everything turns around one centre, and each road's centre is its partner's half. The mirror lives at the pivot.

Two rulers built from the same pair of numbers, one by adding, one by subtracting. Each ruler's centre is exactly the other ruler's half length. Move the sliders; the mirror never breaks.

Fold each gear's journey in half: it creases exactly on one of its own tick years. 478 for green, 1549 for brass.

The Chapter Ring

Leftovers

In the watch: the chapter ring. Twelve positions to share 956 years among; the eight that would not fit are why the hand never comes home clean.

The sky showed you the misfit earlier: 12.37 moons that will not fit inside a year. Here is the mathematical piece of it, small enough to hold. Share 956 candies among children and the division almost never comes out clean. The interesting part is always what stays on the table. Drag the divisor.

The brass gear's journey, 1071 to 2027: twelve equal pieces of 79 years, and 8 years that refuse to fit. The leftover is the engine.

The Case

Perfect Numbers

In the watch: the case. A perfect number is a perfectly cased movement: the sum of its parts equals exactly itself. Nothing missing, nothing spare.

Take a number's parts, everything that divides into it, and stack them up. Almost always the tower misses. Four times in the first eight thousand numbers, the tower lands exactly.

A 496 year stretch of road rebuilt from nothing but its own parts, exactly. Perfection, with no date attached.
The Aperture

One Year, Two Clocks

In the watch: the year window. Change the printed number by one and the movement behind it must change gear: 3096 obeys the 12, 3097 obeys the 19.

Numbers that live next door share nothing: change an amount by one and its entire fingerprint of atoms is replaced. My road measures 3096 = 12 × 258, and the zodiac clock closes exactly. One more year is 3097 = 19 × 163, and the moon's treaty number takes over. The ledger is exact either way. The moonlight is not: across 163 of the moon's cycles the sky leaks fourteen days. Knowing which one you are holding is the whole discipline. Walk the number line around 3096.

The same road, one more year: the 12 clock dies and the 19 clock takes over.
The nearby pattern has an engine. The deep pattern has a bookkeeper. Wisdom is knowing which one you are looking at.

The Testimony

The Vow

The mathematics pauses for one page. What follows is not a theorem,
and no null model can touch it. It is mine.

My name is Ömer Faruk Saatçıoğlu. In my language the name means the watchmaker's son, the son of the keeper of the hours. And it was not always ours: my father chose it, deliberately, changing our family name from Ğayır in 1976. The model already knew that year before I told it: 1976 is the exact midpoint of the century this page runs on, the hinge between its physics half and its silicon half. My father renamed us for the wheel's own trade, half a century before the brass wheel lands. And I put a watch at the heart of this page before I remembered what my own name says.

I found that logic and faith can live side by side. I walked logic as far as logic goes, to the last exact digit of it, and where it stopped, faith kept walking. That is my finding, and it is beyond anything I hoped for when I picked up the pencil: for me, faith will always outrun logic, because logic can only map the wheel. Faith is what rides it. And the map itself, the pure mathematics, is the most faithful thing I have ever touched.

We will always be tested in our reasoning and in our faith. Both tests are one test. I intend to pass it.
